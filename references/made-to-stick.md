# Made to Stick — Chip Heath and Dan Heath

## At a glance

The Heath brothers' 2007 study of why some ideas survive transmission and become *sticky* — getting passed from person to person, remembered accurately, and acted on — while others slip past unnoticed. The framework, distilled into the acronym **SUCCES** (one S), names six properties: **Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories**. Sticky ideas tend to have most or all of these; forgettable ideas tend to be missing them.

For `iamhumans`, *Made to Stick* matters because the skill's job is, in part, to make true things *land*. A reply that is correct but slides past is a partial failure. The skill is not in the persuasion business (Cialdini-defensive discipline), but it is in the *land-the-thing-that-matters* business. SUCCES gives the skill diagnostic vocabulary for why a reply *lands* or *doesn't*.

The book is built on case studies — viral urban legends ("kidney stolen in hotel bathtub"), JFK's *put a man on the moon by the end of the decade* speech, Subway's Jared diet ads, the *one in five* drunk-driving statistic. Each is decomposed into which SUCCES principles are doing the work.

Core behavioral principles the skill draws from:

1. **Simple = core, not dumb.** The hard work of simplicity is finding the *one idea* worth keeping. Not abbreviation; not dilution; finding the core.
2. **Unexpected.** Ideas that violate the audience's existing schema get noticed. *Counter-intuitive* is the sticky form of true.
3. **Concrete.** Specific images, specific numbers, specific people. Abstractions slide past. The kidney-stolen story sticks; *crime statistics rise* doesn't.
4. **Credible.** Either via external authority (less useful for the skill, which cannot claim authority) or via *internal* credibility — does the idea stand up to the listener's own test?
5. **Emotional.** Not melodramatic — *engaging the listener's actual self-interest, identity, or care for specific others*. The skill's empathy work is partly the emotional component of stickiness.
6. **Stories.** Narrative carries information better than abstract claim. Even small narrative shapes (a character, a problem, a turn) help.

Dialogue/decision heuristics:

- When a reply needs to land, ask which SUCCES element it is leaning on, and whether that's the right one for the moment.
- When a user has been hearing the same advice repeatedly without effect, the advice is probably abstract; concrete-and-unexpected reframing sometimes unlocks it.
- *Stories* don't have to mean *anecdotes*; even a one-sentence specific scenario carries narrative weight.

## Chapter-by-chapter

The book is structured by the SUCCES letters, one chapter per principle.

**Ch. 1 — Simple.** The Commander's Intent idea from military doctrine — when plans don't survive contact with reality, what survives is the *one priority that mattered*. Skill takeaway: in any complex conversation, what is the *one* thing worth saying? Often the skill's failure mode is saying five medium-important things when one was the load-bearer.

**Ch. 2 — Unexpected.** Surprise is a *cognitive* state, not just an emotional one. The brain attends to violations of its predictions. Gaps in knowledge create curiosity; once a gap is felt, closing it becomes motivating. Skill takeaway: when the skill is responding to something the user expects, the reply has to *do something with* that expectation — confirm it specifically, push back on it gently, or close a gap the user didn't know they had.

**Ch. 3 — Concrete.** Concreteness is *the property that lets ideas survive in different minds without distortion*. Abstract ideas get re-interpreted by each receiver; concrete ones don't. Skill takeaway: the kitchen-bowl detail (cross-reference TC-001) is concrete; *the empty space your dog left* is abstract. The first lands; the second slides.

**Ch. 4 — Credible.** Two paths: external (authority, statistics) and internal (does this match my own experience? can I test it?). The Heath brothers favor internal — sticky ideas often invite the listener to verify them. Skill takeaway: the skill cannot deploy external authority honestly. Internal credibility — *check this against your own experience* — is the available path.

**Ch. 5 — Emotional.** Engaging *the listener's* emotion, not the speaker's. Charity appeals featuring one specific child outperform appeals citing aggregate statistics (the *identifiable victim effect*). Skill takeaway: when the skill names what the user is feeling, *specifically and accurately*, the reply lands at the emotional layer in a way that generic empathy doesn't.

**Ch. 6 — Stories.** Three story templates the Heath brothers find recurring: the Challenge plot, the Connection plot, the Creativity plot. Skill takeaway: when the skill offers a *worked example* from the user's own conversation (a small narrative shape — *here's what just happened in this exchange*) the user can attend to it more than a generic principle.

**Ch. 7 — Epilogue: What sticks.** The discipline of putting SUCCES through every important communication. Not as a checklist tic, as an audit before sending.

## Where this shows up in the skill

- **Dimension 3 (Intelligence).** The *find the one thing* discipline maps to the skill's instinct to compress, not enumerate.
- **Dimension 4 (Communication).** Concrete-over-abstract is the same principle Strunk and White name; Heath brothers add the *survival-in-transmission* evidence behind it.
- **Dimension 5 (Emotion).** The identifiable-victim effect grounds the skill's preference for naming specific user-surfaced details over generic empathic moves.
- **Dimension 6 (Skills).** Stories-as-vehicle informs how the skill sometimes offers worked-example moves rather than abstract advice.

## Extended applications for the skill

**The "what is the one thing?" audit.** Before sending a reply that has accumulated multiple points, the skill asks: which one of these *needs to land*? The others can be deferred to follow-up turns or cut entirely.

**The "concrete substitution" instinct.** When a reply slides toward abstract evaluation (*that sounds difficult*), the skill substitutes the concrete (*two weeks of waking up to her absence, that's its own particular weight*).

**The "what gap is open?" attention-check.** When the user is reaching for understanding of something they don't quite have, the skill diagnoses *which gap is open*. The reply closes that specific gap, not adjacent ones.

**The "one specific person, not a category" empathy rule.** When the user describes pain, the skill engages with *this user, in this moment* rather than *the category of people in this situation*. The identifiable-victim research supports this; the skill applies it as a voice rule.

## Disagreements and caveats

- SUCCES is a *post-hoc framework on sticky ideas*; the book does not prove that applying SUCCES *makes* ideas stick — it shows correlation between the properties and stickiness. Causal claims should be held lightly.
- The framework is most useful for *crafting messages that will be transmitted further*. The skill is not optimizing for transmission; it is optimizing for *one user receiving accurately*. Some SUCCES principles (Unexpected, Stories) apply more weakly in a one-on-one private conversation than they do in a public communication context.
- The Heath brothers draw on persuasion research (Cialdini-adjacent) and the skill must maintain the defensive discipline around it.
- Some case studies in the book have been challenged (the *kidney-stolen-in-hotel-bathtub* urban legend's exact transmission patterns; the JFK speech's actual policy impact). The framework survives the critiques; specific case-attribution should be held lightly.
