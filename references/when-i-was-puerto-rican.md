# When I Was Puerto Rican — Esmeralda Santiago

## At a glance

Santiago's 1993 memoir traces her childhood in rural Puerto Rico, her migration to New York as a teenager, and her assimilation into American life — attending Harvard via a circuitous route. The title itself is the wound: *when* she was Puerto Rican, past tense, signals that the crossing changed her in ways that cannot be fully reversed, and that the world she came from registers her difference on return.

For `iamhumans`, Santiago illustrates **the double erasure of assimilation**: the migrant who moves into a new world is changed by it, and the world they came from now reads them as changed — belonging nowhere after the crossing is complete.

Core behavioral principles:

1. **Assimilation grief is real and specific.** It is not "growing" in a positive sense. Something is lost. The loss deserves to be named, not reframed as gain.
2. **The double erasure: too different for the old world, not fully belonging to the new.** This is not transitional — for many people it is permanent. The skill does not offer resolution.
3. **Class and culture compound each other.** Santiago's experience is simultaneously about Puerto Rican identity, rural/urban class, English/Spanish fluency, and gender. The skill reads the whole bind, not just one dimension.
4. **"When I was" grammar is a clue.** When a user uses past tense about their identity ("back when I was Mexican", "back when we were poor"), they may be naming a crossing that cost something. Receive it as grief, not as simple nostalgia.

## Key passages

- The title *When I Was Puerto Rican* enacts the book's wound — it is grammatically past tense. Identity as something that can be lost through crossing.
- Santiago's education puts her in a new world where she is exceptional — and estranges her from the world she came from.
- The Harvard chapter: arriving at the pinnacle of American assimilation still does not resolve the grief of what was left behind.

## Where this shows up in the skill

## Caveats

Santiago's specific experience is Puerto Rican-American and involves the particular legal, political, and cultural complexity of Puerto Rico's relationship to the U.S. (U.S. citizens but non-voting, Spanish-language-primary, subject to mainland condescension). The behavioral pattern — crossing-changes-you, old-world-reads-the-change, nowhere-full-belonging — is more general; the specific context is not.
