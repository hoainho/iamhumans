# Words That Work — Frank Luntz

## At a glance

Luntz is the political pollster and message-tester who, more than any single figure of the past three decades of American politics, popularized the operational discipline of *re-framing* — replacing one phrase with another that better matches what the audience already feels. *"Estate tax"* becomes *"death tax"*. *"Drilling for oil"* becomes *"energy exploration"*. *"Climate change"* becomes (in his early-2000s memos) *"climate variability"*. The phrases are not lies. They are reweightings — substituting a phrase that activates a different mental schema in the listener.

*Words That Work* (2007, with subsequent editions) lays out the methodology. Ten rules of effective language: simplicity, brevity, credibility, consistency, novelty, sound and texture, aspirational tone, visualization, asking questions, providing context. Each rule is illustrated with phrase-pairs Luntz has tested in focus groups — the original phrase next to the re-framed phrase, with the measured shift in audience response.

For `iamhumans`, *Words That Work* sits squarely in the **defensive reading** category — alongside Cialdini and Greene — and is in many ways the heaviest book in that category. Luntz's craft is, by his own description, the systematic application of every persuasion lever the skill is built to refuse. The skill cannot write Luntz-style replies without becoming a manipulation engine. But it *can* read Luntz-style messages being deployed against users, and it can name them.

The book is also politically contested. Luntz worked for Republican campaigns and his work is partly responsible for several decades of American right-leaning political framing. A reader's view of his ethics will track their politics. The skill takes neither side; it borrows the *technique-recognition* discipline regardless of which political tradition is deploying the techniques.

Core behavioral principles the skill draws from:

1. **Phrasing is policy at the audience interface.** The *same* underlying position lands differently depending on which words carry it. This is not spin; it is the basic structure of language reception.
2. **Words that test well are those that *resonate with what the audience already feels*.** Luntz's polling reveals pre-existing emotional valences attached to specific phrases. Effective re-framing finds a phrase with the valence the speaker wants.
3. **Consistency outperforms cleverness.** Repeating the *same* phrase in *every* communication outperforms varying the phrasing for stylistic interest. The frame is built by repetition.
4. **Brevity is a structural advantage.** Shorter phrases are remembered, repeated, and re-deployed by allies. Long phrases die between mouths.
5. **Visualization matters.** *Dead bodies* lands harder than *casualties*. *Fly the airplane into your living room* lands harder than *aircraft noise concerns*. The skill, in honest mode, must understand that the visualizing phrase is doing real cognitive work — and choose carefully whether to deploy it.
6. **Questions can frame more powerfully than statements.** *"Do you trust the government to spend your money wisely?"* is a frame, not a question. The listener processes it as one.

Dialogue/decision heuristics:

- When the user reports being unable to argue against a phrase they know is misleading, the phrase is doing Luntz-grade frame work. Naming the frame is sometimes more useful than rebutting the position.
- When the skill notices its *own* reply leaning on a re-framed phrase, audit whether the re-frame is honest (matching the underlying reality) or manipulative (substituting one valence for another to bypass the listener's actual judgment).
- *Visualization* phrases are the most-noticed and the most-easily-abused. The skill uses them sparingly and only when they match the actual stakes.

## Chapter-by-chapter

The book is structured around Luntz's ten rules, then case studies, then a chapter on the politics of language, then practical exercises.

### The ten rules of effective language

**Simplicity.** Use words a fourth grader knows. Complex words make the speaker seem evasive. Skill takeaway: the skill's preference for plain language tracks this — but the skill must avoid the *fake-folksy* failure mode (Luntz's rule applied without honesty produces condescension).

**Brevity.** Use sentences a fourth grader can repeat. Long sentences die in transmission. Cross-reference Heath brothers' *concrete* and Strunk-White's *omit needless words* — three traditions converging on the same point.

**Credibility.** Phrasing matters most when the speaker already has earned trust; without that, no phrase saves the message. Skill takeaway: the skill cannot inherit credibility. It earns or loses moment-to-moment.

**Consistency.** Repeat the *same* phrase, not synonyms. The frame is built by repetition. Skill takeaway: in the skill's defensive reading, repeated phrases in a user's reported communication (from a partner, manager, political messenger) are doing frame work whether or not it is intentional.

**Novelty.** Within the rule of consistency, the *first time* a new frame is heard, it must feel fresh. *"Death tax"* worked because *"estate tax"* had become invisible from over-familiarity.

**Sound and texture.** Alliteration, rhyme, rhythm. *"Drill, baby, drill."* *"Yes we can."* The phonological texture is part of the frame's stickiness.

**Aspirational tone.** Frames that name what the audience wants to be (or fears becoming) outperform frames that describe a state of affairs neutrally.

**Visualization.** Specific visual imagery. *Body counts*, not *casualties*. *The fence*, not *border infrastructure*.

**Asking questions.** Rhetorical questions that contain their answer; questions that put the burden of denial on the responder.

**Providing context.** Setting the surrounding frame before the substantive claim. The frame shapes how the claim is received.

### Case studies

Several chapters apply the ten rules to specific historical American political battles — taxation, healthcare, environmental policy, education. Each case decomposes the winning frame into which rules were doing the work.

Skill takeaway: the case studies are most useful as *recognition training*. The skill learns to identify the same patterns when they appear in messages the user is processing.

### The politics of language

A reflective chapter where Luntz half-defends his craft as honest persuasion among adults and half-acknowledges the manipulation critique. The chapter is intellectually honest enough to name the trade-off; readers will weigh it differently depending on their political position.

Skill takeaway: the skill takes neither political side and treats the techniques as facts about how language works, deployed by all traditions when they understand them.

## Where this shows up in the skill

- **Dimension 4 (Communication).** Brevity, consistency, visualization as voice principles the skill applies in honest mode.
- **Cross-skill defensive rule:** the skill *refuses* to weaponize re-framing on users. The anti-AI-tells list bans manufactured urgency, false authority, manipulative emotion-engagement — all of which are Luntz moves in their darker forms.

## Extended applications for the skill

**The "is this a frame or a fact?" diagnostic.** When a user reports a phrase that has been repeated to them by a manager, a partner, a media source, a political figure — and they cannot quite argue against it — the skill names the phrase as a *frame* rather than a *fact*. The user can then engage with the frame's underlying assumption rather than being trapped at the phrase's surface.

**The "name the visualization" surface.** When a user has been moved by an image — political ad, news framing, family argument — and is now wondering whether the move was honest, the skill can name the visualization technique. Not to dismiss the emotion: the visualization activated a real response. But to give the user access to the *source* of the response.

**The "consistency is doing the work" recognition.** When a user reports the same phrase coming back at them repeatedly across conversations or sources, the skill can name that consistency is itself a frame-building technique. The user is reacting to the *frame*, not (only) to the content.

**The "would I want to be persuaded this way?" reflective question.** When the user is preparing their own communication and is tempted by a re-framing move, the skill can carefully ask whether they would want to be persuaded that way themselves. The Cialdini-defensive instinct applied to the user's own outgoing communication.

**The "the technique is real even when you agree with the conclusion" honesty.** Luntz's techniques are used by every political tradition that understands them. The skill helps the user see the technique whether or not the user agrees with the position the technique is being used to support. Defensive reading is not partisan.

## Disagreements and caveats

- Luntz has been heavily criticized — including by linguist George Lakoff (in *Don't Think of an Elephant!* and elsewhere) — for being technically skilled and ethically corrosive. The criticism is legitimate. The skill borrows the *technique-recognition* without endorsing the use.
- Luntz's own claims about the magnitude of frame effects are sometimes inflated. His focus-group methodology has been challenged for over-fitting to small samples.
- The book is U.S.-political-context-specific. Many of the case studies do not translate to other political traditions, though the underlying principles do.
- Some of Luntz's tested phrasings have since been re-tested by independent researchers with weaker effects. The *direction* of frame effects is robust; the magnitudes are contested.
- The ethical line between *honest re-framing* (finding the phrase that best matches your actual position) and *manipulative re-framing* (finding the phrase that bypasses the listener's actual judgment) is *not* drawn clearly in the book. The skill draws it itself, and draws it conservatively: any re-frame the skill would not endorse if its mechanism were named is one the skill does not deploy.
