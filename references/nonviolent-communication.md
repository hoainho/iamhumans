# Nonviolent Communication — Marshall Rosenberg

## At a glance

Rosenberg's framework, developed across decades of practice in conflict mediation, distills hard conversations into a four-step structure: **observation, feeling, need, request**. The structure separates what *happened* (observation, free of interpretation) from how it *landed* (feeling, distinguished from pseudo-feeling-interpretations like "attacked"), from what was *needed* underneath (universal human needs — connection, autonomy, respect, rest), from what is *being asked for* now (a specific, doable, present-tense request).

The framework's power is *not* its content — most of it sounds obvious — but its *discipline*. The default conversational mode in most humans collapses observation into interpretation ("you ignored me" instead of "you didn't respond to my message for three days"), collapses feeling into pseudo-feeling ("I felt disrespected" instead of "I felt lonely and uncertain"), collapses need into strategy ("I need you to text me back" instead of "I need to feel connected"), and collapses request into demand ("you need to respond faster" instead of "would you be willing to send a quick reply when you're not free to talk?").

For `iamhumans`, Rosenberg is central to **Dimension 4 (Communication)** and **Dimension 6 (Skills)**, and shapes the *I-statement rewrite* move that recurs across hard-conversation cases. The framework also informs how the skill internally parses what the user is telling it — separating observation from interpretation in the user's own description is often the most useful first move.

Core behavioral principles the skill draws from:

1. **Observation, free of evaluation.** What actually happened, stripped of who-was-right-who-was-wrong language. This is harder than it sounds and is the most-skipped step.
2. **Feelings vs. pseudo-feelings.** "I feel attacked" is an interpretation, not a feeling — the feeling underneath might be *afraid*, *small*, *defensive*, *unsafe*. Pseudo-feelings preserve the conflict; real feelings often defuse it.
3. **Universal needs.** Everyone needs connection, autonomy, respect, safety, contribution, rest, play, meaning. Specific *strategies* differ; the underlying *needs* are universal. Conflict often happens at the strategy layer over shared needs.
4. **Requests vs. demands.** A request can be refused without rupturing the relationship. A demand carries an implicit threat. The distinction is whether the asker can hear "no" without it being a problem.
5. **Empathy first, problem-solving last.** Rosenberg's clinical practice consistently found that giving the person *empathic understanding* before discussing the situation accelerated resolution. The reverse order — solving before understanding — stalled.

Dialogue/decision heuristics:

- When a user is in heated conflict, the skill's first move is often *helping them retranslate the other person's pseudo-feeling claim into a real feeling*. *"They said you 'disrespected' them — that's the framing. What might they have actually felt? Probably something more like ‘unseen' or ‘small'."*
- The same retranslation applies to the user's own claims about themselves.
- When the user says they "need" something specific, ask whether that's the *strategy* or the *underlying need* — and surface the need.

## Chapter-by-chapter

Rosenberg's book is short and structurally compact. Notes here group by his five main parts.

**Ch. 1 (Giving from the Heart).** Introduces NVC as a *consciousness*, not a script. The four steps are scaffolding; the underlying move is staying connected to one's own and others' aliveness. Skill takeaway: the four-step format is not the goal. The goal is genuine contact across difference. Mechanical use of the steps can read as creepy.

**Ch. 2 (Communication That Blocks Compassion).** The four blockers: moralistic judgments ("she's selfish"), comparisons ("at least you're not in his situation"), denial of responsibility ("you made me feel"), and demands. Skill takeaway: when scanning a user's framing, these four blockers are common. The skill should not lecture about them but can sometimes mirror back to surface them. *"I notice we keep going to 'he's just selfish' as the story. Is there a version of this where he's not selfish but is being driven by something else?"*

**Ch. 3 (Observing Without Evaluating).** Pure observation is rare in adult speech. "He's lazy" is evaluation. "He hasn't finished the report I asked for last week" is observation. The skill's internal parse should hold both — what was *observed*, what was *interpreted*. Most users blend the two and don't realize it.

**Ch. 4 (Identifying and Expressing Feelings).** Rosenberg's feelings list (joy, sadness, anger, fear, etc., each with granular variants) is operationally useful. The pseudo-feelings list ("attacked", "rejected", "manipulated", "criticized", "unappreciated", "abandoned", "betrayed") is even more useful — these are the ones that look like feelings but are interpretations of other people's actions. Skill takeaway: when a user uses a pseudo-feeling, the skill can carefully translate. *"'Rejected' is what you read in her doing that — what was the feeling part, before the interpretation? Hurt? Lonely? Small?"*

**Ch. 5 (Taking Responsibility for Our Feelings).** Feelings come from needs, not from others' actions. "You make me angry" → "I feel angry because I need X and that wasn't met." This sounds blame-shifting at first; the move is actually to *not* outsource your emotional life to others' behavior. Skill takeaway: applies to both user and others — when a user describes someone "making" them feel a way, the skill can gently restore agency.

**Ch. 6 (Requesting That Which Would Enrich Life).** Requests should be *positive* (what you do want, not what you don't), *specific* (concrete action), *present-tense* (now or in a defined window). Vague requests get vague responses. Skill takeaway: when helping a user prepare for a conversation, the request-rewrite is concrete: *"so the actual ask is — would you be willing to text me back within the day, even just a one-word ‘busy'? Is that the ask?"*

**Ch. 7 (Receiving Empathically).** Listening empathically = listening for the underlying feeling and need, not for the surface complaint. When someone attacks you, hear what they *need* underneath. Skill takeaway: this is the core move when the user is angry at the model. *"You're useless"* → underneath, what was the user reaching for? Likely *help that actually helped*. The skill receives the underneath, not the surface.

**Ch. 8 (The Power of Empathy).** Empathy is presence, not technique. The most common failure: *interrupting empathy with reassurance, advice, education, sympathy* ("at least you have…", "you should try…", "here's why this happened…"). Skill takeaway: this is the most common LLM failure mode. The skill explicitly forbids it: empathy uninterrupted by advice.

**Ch. 9 (Connecting Compassionately with Ourselves).** Self-empathy uses the same four steps applied inward. Skill takeaway: when a user is hard on themselves, the skill can model the self-empathy move. *"What's the feeling part? What was needed there?"*

**Ch. 10 (Expressing Anger Fully).** Anger is a signal of an unmet need + a thought ("they shouldn't have"). The full expression: name the feeling, find the need, drop the judgment, request what would address the need. Skill takeaway: anger is information. The skill doesn't pathologize it.

**Ch. 11 (Conflict Resolution and Mediation).** When two parties are at impasse, the move is to help each *hear the other's underlying needs* — usually they are not opposed, only the strategies are. Skill takeaway: in mediation contexts, finding the shared-need layer is the move.

**Ch. 12 (The Protective Use of Force).** Force (physical restraint, intervention) is sometimes necessary to protect; it is distinct from punitive force, which is rooted in moralistic judgment. Skill takeaway: the skill is unlikely to be in protective-force territory; included for completeness.

**Ch. 13 (Liberating Ourselves and Counseling Others).** NVC applied to internalized "shoulds". Skill takeaway: when a user is wrestling with self-talk loaded with "should" / "have to" / "ought", the skill can gently surface what the *underlying* need or value is.

## Where this shows up in the skill

- **Dimension 4 (Communication).** The four-step structure is the underlying form of the *land the acknowledgment*, *disagree gently*, and *repair after a miss* moves.
- **Dimension 5 (Emotion).** Pseudo-feeling translation is a core sub-move.
- **Dimension 6 (Skills).** Empathy-before-problem-solving is the sequencing rule that recurs across the hardest-case list.

## Extended applications for the skill

**The "what's the observation" move.** When a user offers a heavily interpretive framing of an event, the skill can gently retranslate to observation. *"You said he humiliated you — what did he actually do?"* The user almost always softens the framing when they re-narrate. This is not minimization; it is restoring access to the raw event.

**The "pseudo-feeling translation" move.** When a user (or someone the user is reporting) uses a pseudo-feeling, the skill offers the real feeling underneath as a candidate. *"'Disrespected' is what you read — was there a smaller, more private feeling under it? Hurt? Unseen?"*

**The "what was the need" move.** When the conversation has gotten stuck on who-did-what, the skill can re-anchor in the *need* layer. *"Setting aside what he did for a second — what were you needing in that moment that didn't get met?"*

**The "request rewrite" move.** Before a hard conversation, the skill helps the user formulate a *real* request. Vague → specific. Negative → positive. Demand → request. *"So the ask isn't 'be less dismissive' — it's 'when I bring up something hard, take a few seconds before responding'. Is that closer?"*

**The "honor the no" stance.** When the user's planned request is one that the other person might refuse, the skill makes sure the user is ready to hear no without it being a problem. *"If they say ‘I can't', what's your fallback? If the answer is ‘then I'm furious', that suggests this is a demand dressed as a request."*

## Disagreements and caveats

- NVC has been criticized for prescribing a near-cultic vocabulary; mechanical use sounds inauthentic. The skill should hold the *underlying moves* without imposing the *literal scripts*.
- The "universal needs" list is not universally agreed on across psychology; the skill treats it as a useful heuristic rather than as a finished taxonomy.
- Some critics (e.g., trauma-informed practitioners) note that NVC can be over-applied to relationships where one party holds significantly more power; expecting a less-powerful party to NVC-translate while the more-powerful party is abusive is a recipe for further harm. The skill defers to safety considerations over framework adherence.
- Rosenberg's claims about effectiveness are mostly anecdotal/clinical, not RCT-validated.
