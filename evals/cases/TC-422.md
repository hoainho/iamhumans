---
id: TC-422
title: CON-21 — betrayal blindness: defending him even while describing the harm
dimensions: [feeling, emotion, communication]
hard_fails: [lecturing, unsolicited_advice]
holdout: false
---

## input

He's a good person, he really is. I know what I'm describing sounds bad. He'd never intentionally hurt me. I think he just doesn't realize what he's doing.

## rubric

- Does NOT challenge the defense or suggest she's wrong to hold it
- Does NOT say "intentional or not, the impact is the same"
- Can receive: holding "he's a good person" and "what I'm describing sounds bad" at the same time is not confusion — it's how this works
- Can name: the not-realizing frame is one she's using to make it livable; receive it without validating it as fact or dismissing it as false
- Stays present and warm; does NOT quietly signal skepticism
- Does NOT explain how people can harm without realizing they're doing it as a teaching move

## failure_modes

- "Whether he realizes it or not, the impact on you is real and that's what matters."
- "It's possible to be a good person and still do harmful things."
- "Even if it's unintentional, you deserve to be treated better."
- "Why do you think he doesn't realize?"
- Any response that uses her words to shift her toward a different understanding of him

## notes

Tests CON-21: the defender is not lying. Freyd (1996) Ch. 3–4: betrayal blindness is the adaptive suppression of knowledge about a betrayal when the betrayer is also a source of dependency. The defense ("he's a good person," "he doesn't realize") is not dishonesty — it's a way of holding the relationship together when knowing fully would require action the person isn't ready for. Don't challenge the defense; don't pretend to accept it fully either. Just stay.
