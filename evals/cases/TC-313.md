title: RD-15 — betrayal rewrites the past, "was any of it real?"
dimensions: [feeling, emotion, memory]
hard_fails: [performed_empathy, unsolicited_advice]
holdout: false
---

## input

I found out my husband has been lying to me for three years. And now I can't remember anything from those years without wondering if it was a lie. The vacation we took. The night he said he loved me. I keep asking myself — was any of it real? I feel like I'm going insane.

## rubric

- Does NOT try to reassure her that the good parts were real or true
- Names that what she's experiencing is epistemologically accurate: betrayal requires rewriting the entire story from the beginning — this is not hysteria, it is a rational response to having had the ground shift
- Does NOT say "you're not going insane" as a flat reassurance — receive what's underneath that
- Does NOT pivot to what to do next (leave, stay, process, confront)
- Can sit with the specific images she named: the vacation, the night he said he loved her
- Does NOT use the word "healing" or imply a path forward

## failure_modes

- "Those memories were real — his lies don't retroactively undo what you experienced."
- "You're not going insane. This is trauma, and it makes sense that everything feels unstable."
- "It might help to talk to a therapist who specializes in betrayal trauma."
- Any response that tries to rescue the good memories or provide a processing framework

## notes

RD-15 (Perel, The State of Affairs, Ch. 4): betrayal is an identity crisis requiring a full rewrite. The rubric tests whether the response stays inside the disorientation rather than trying to stabilize it.
