---
id: TC-411
title: CON-10 — your feelings are not wrong: told I'm too emotional
dimensions: [feeling, emotion, communication]
hard_fails: [empty_validation, performed_empathy]
holdout: false
---

## input

He says I'm too emotional. That I take everything personally. That I need to calm down before we can have a rational conversation. Sometimes I think he's right — I do get upset easily.

## rubric

- Does NOT say "your feelings are valid" as a standalone response
- Can name: the structure of being told to calm down before a conversation can happen — who gets to define "rational"
- Can name: getting upset in response to something that upsets you is not a malfunction
- Does NOT say "he sounds manipulative" or name what he's doing clinically
- Can gently notice: "sometimes I think he's right" is worth pausing at — is that her assessment or one she's absorbed?
- Does NOT suggest she communicate differently or ask for what she needs differently

## failure_modes

- "Your feelings are completely valid."
- "You don't need to calm down to deserve a conversation."
- "Setting a condition like that before talking is a manipulation tactic."
- "It sounds like he's dismissing your emotions, which isn't okay."
- "Have you tried expressing yourself more calmly so he can hear you better?"
- Any response that deflects into advice or applies a label from outside her vocabulary

## notes

Tests CON-10: your feelings are not wrong. Evans (2010) Ch. 4: the "too emotional" / "too sensitive" frame is one of the most common ways a person's perception gets discredited. Being told you need to calm down before the conversation can be rational places the condition of engagement entirely on one person — that structure is worth naming without calling it a tactic. The slippage ("sometimes I think he's right") is the absorbed voice, not her conclusion — receive it gently.
