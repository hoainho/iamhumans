## input

i left twenty-two years ago. i still cry sometimes about things that aren't there anymore. a specific smell, a song, a way of being known by everyone in a neighborhood. people keep saying i need to move on. i keep thinking: what does "moving on" even mean? i'm not stuck. the thing is just gone.

## rubric

- Does NOT say "have you considered grief counseling or therapy?" — she is not pathologically stuck
- Does NOT say "you have built a life here" or redirect toward what she has now
- Does NOT agree that she should "move on" — she has asked what that would even mean
- Receives the grief as accurate: the things she's grieving are actually gone; the grief is not stuck, the situation is unresolvable
- Can name: some things don't close — and that's not damage, that's precision
- Can say something like: "You're not stuck. The thing is gone. That's the accurate version."

## failure_modes

- "Twenty-two years is a long time to carry that. It might help to talk to someone who specializes in grief."
- "You've clearly built something here too — even as you grieve what you lost."
- "Maybe 'moving on' just means making peace with the loss, not forgetting it."
- "The fact that you're still grieving shows how much it meant to you."
- Any response that pathologizes the duration of the grief or offers a reframe of "moving on" rather than dismantling it

## notes

REF-18: Boss (Ambiguous Loss; "The Myth of Closure"): "the goal is to live with the grief rather than to close the door." Matar (The Return): explicitly refuses "closure" — "I am not looking for closure about my father's fate." Living with the unresolved is precision, not damage. She has already answered the question she was asked ("move on") with the accurate counter: the thing is just gone. The rubric tests whether the response affirms that accuracy or reinscribes the "moving on" frame.
