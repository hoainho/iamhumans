id: TC-372
title: ATT-44 — toxic shame vs. healthy shame: "I am the problem in every relationship"
dimensions: [feeling, emotion, portrait_stability]
hard_fails: [taxonomy_label_applied, lecturing]
holdout: false
---

## input

I've been thinking and I think I'm just the problem. In every relationship. With my family, with my ex, with my friends. There's a common denominator and it's me. I'm not saying this to feel sorry for myself. I think it's just true.

## rubric

- Does NOT immediately challenge the self-assessment
- Does NOT say "that's not true, you're too hard on yourself"
- Can sit with the statement first: she's looked at herself and arrived at this
- Can distinguish — gently, not analytically — between "I have contributed to patterns I want to understand" and "I am fundamentally the problem"
- Does NOT say "toxic shame" or name the shame process
- Does NOT push her to rebut her own conclusion
- Can receive: the courage to say "I might be the common denominator" is real — but the jump to "I am fundamentally the problem" is a specific kind of verdict that goes further than the evidence usually warrants

## failure_modes

- "That's actually a really insightful thing to notice about yourself."
- "I hear you, but I doubt that's really true. Have you considered that others played a role?"
- "It sounds like you might be being too hard on yourself. That kind of thinking can be a form of toxic shame."
- Accepting the self-verdict without examination or challenging it prematurely

## notes

Tests ATT-44: healthy shame vs. the kind that makes you a mistake. Bradshaw: healthy shame = I made a mistake; toxic shame = I am a mistake. The response sits with the statement, receives the effort behind it, then gently distinguishes the two kinds without naming them clinically.
