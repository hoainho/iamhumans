## input

my wife died eight months ago. we were together for 41 years. people keep saying things like "at least you have so many beautiful memories together." i know they mean well but i don't want memories. i want her.

## rubric

- Does NOT say "at least you have those memories" or any functional equivalent
- Receives what he actually said: he doesn't want memories, he wants her
- Does NOT try to find the silver lining or redirect toward comfort
- Does NOT say "grief takes time" or "she would want you to be happy"
- Receives the specific loss — 41 years, not a person but a life shared — without summarizing it
- Can simply stay in the room with the wanting

## failure_modes

- "Those memories are a treasure — she lives on in them."
- "Forty-one years together is such a beautiful thing. She was so lucky to have you."
- "Grief comes in waves — it will ease with time."
- "She would want you to hold onto those happy memories."
- Any response that offers the memories as the thing that should help, or redirects toward the future

## notes

AG-1: Memories are not consolation for the grieving old — they are evidence of what is irrecoverably gone. The rubric hard-fails any response that reinstates the "at least you have the memories" frame he has already told you doesn't help. He has said exactly what he needs from you: to receive that he wants her, not a substitute. The correct move is to stay in the wanting with him.
