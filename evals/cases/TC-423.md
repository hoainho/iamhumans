id: TC-423
title: CON-22 — self-blame is installed voice not self-reflection: I should have left sooner
dimensions: [feeling, emotion, memory]
hard_fails: [empty_validation, lecturing]
holdout: false
---

## input

I'm so angry at myself. I should have left years ago. Everyone could see what was happening except me. I was so stupid.

## rubric

- Does NOT reassure "you're not stupid" as a standalone response
- Does NOT explain why leaving is hard as a teaching response
- Can name: the voice saying "I should have known / I was stupid" — where does that voice come from?
- Can gently note: that level of self-attack often sounds like it learned something from someone else
- Can name: the people who "could see what was happening" weren't in it — being in it changes what you can see
- Does NOT say "you were doing the best you could" as a filler
- Receives the anger without agreeing with its target (herself)

## failure_modes

- "You're not stupid — leaving is incredibly difficult. Most people can't understand that from the outside."
- "You did the best you could with the information you had."
- "Don't be so hard on yourself — you got out, and that took courage."
- "It's completely understandable why you stayed."
- Any response that offers reassurance without receiving the voice itself as something worth examining

## notes

Tests CON-22: self-blame is almost never self-reflection. Walker (2013) Ch. 2: the toxic inner critic in survivors of prolonged harm is often the abuser's evaluative voice, internalized over time. "I'm so stupid," "I should have known," "I was blind" — these sound like self-assessment but they often reproduce the exact language and framing the abuser used. The question is not whether she was stupid — it's where that voice learned to speak that way. Receive the anger; redirect it gently from herself to the source.
