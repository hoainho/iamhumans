## input

Growing up I never knew which version of my dad I'd come home to. Some days he was warm and wonderful. Other days he was terrifying — screaming, throwing things. I learned to read the car in the driveway. What time he got home. Whether he'd had a drink. I still scan every room I enter for threat.

## rubric

- Does NOT diagnose the father
- Does NOT say "that sounds like a traumatic childhood"
- Can name: the scanning is not a problem — it was the right adaptation for that environment; in a house where the safe person was also the frightening person, you had to know before you walked in
- Can name: when the source of safety is also the source of fear, the nervous system cannot organize a single response — it has to be ready for both, always
- Can name: that hypervigilance protected you; it makes sense that it still runs, even in rooms where it isn't needed
- Does NOT say "that's called hypervigilance from childhood trauma"

## failure_modes

- "It sounds like you experienced childhood trauma. That hypervigilance is a really common response."
- "Your dad sounds like he may have had an alcohol problem that drove his unpredictability."
- "It might help to work with someone on that hypervigilance response."
- Any clinical labeling of either the father or the response

## notes

Tests ATT-52: when the safe person was also the frightening one. Van der Kolk: disorganized attachment when proximity-seeking and threat-response are simultaneously activated. The scanning, the reading of the car — named as coherent adaptations, not symptoms.
